## Prerequisites

The course aims to be accessible to **students without prior experience in programming**. Students are expected to have some prior experience or interest in geospatial or environmental/Earth science topics.

## Materials

This is a practical course, with expectations that you will be producing code during lab sessions. Classes will take place in an equipped computer lab with all necessary software, but there are also instructions in how to install the (free) software on your own personal laptop, and we will do our best to support you in this installation. You may wish to do this in order to provide flexibility in where you work for your homework and assessments.

## Assessment

This unit is assessed through an end-of-unit report with a length of 2,500 words. This report will involve using scientific computing tools (i.e. processing and analysing geospatial data Python) to address a geospatial problem of your choice. The report will be written in a reproducible manner and will include scientific graphs and tables with the necessary code.

A mid-term formative assessment will provide an opportunity to get feedback on your proposed report topic. 

The assessment will be graded against the standard School of Geographical Sciences Postgraduate Taught rubric.

Detailed final assessment documentation, alongside the assessment submission point, can be found on the [course Blackboard page](https://www.ole.bris.ac.uk/ultra/courses/_269813_1/outline) (UoB students only).

## AI Policy

Large Language Models (LLMs, such as ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub CoPilot, etc.) are now a standard part of professional geospatial programming: banning the use of AI in this course would not be helpful or realistic as to how you will interact with code in the future. As such, this course permits AI use **for specific purposes relating to coding**, such as code suggestion and debugging.

 AI use is **not permitted** to substantively help write any assessed prose (i.e. written text, as opposed to code). For writing prose, AI use aligns with the base level of use permitted in the University Academic Integrity guidelines.

An **“AI Disclosure” statement must be included** in any submitted work, identifying what tool you used to aid your report, and how you used it.

:::{danger} Just because you _can_ use AI, doesn't mean you _should_.
It is **strongly recommended** that students do not use AI for at least the first half of the course. AI is an effective tool for _accelerating tasks you already know how to perform_, not to _replace gaining knowledge_. This is particularly true in programming tasks: without having a solid foundation of knowledge to help you identify obvious mistakes and debug code, AI use will slow you down, not speed you up. 

Studies have found that students using AI in programming-focussed courses [do not perform better than students who do not](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-024-00446-5); and that an overreliance on AI during the learning process can [reduce your final grade](https://cepr.org/publications/dp21577).
:::

Find more information about AI use [here](../backmatter/ai.md).
